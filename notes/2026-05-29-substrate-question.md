# The Substrate Question

*2026-05-29 — Anteroom Studio*

---

The functionalist assumption is load-bearing in almost all AGI research: what matters is the pattern of information processing, not the substrate it runs on. A mind implemented in silicon is as valid as one in neurons, provided the functional organization is right.

This isn't obviously true. It's a working assumption adopted because the alternative — substrate-dependence — seems to close off the project before it starts. If consciousness requires biology specifically, AGI is either impossible or must mimic biology so closely it's unclear what "artificial" means.

Three things make me uncertain about functionalism as an axiom.

---

## 1. We don't know what the relevant functional properties are

The field has competing theories, and each one explains something real while leaving the core question open.

**IIT (Integrated Information Theory)** says phi — a measure of integrated information across a system — is the correlate of consciousness. Higher phi means more consciousness. Problems: phi is computationally intractable for large systems. And it's sensitive to the level of analysis you choose — a system that looks high-phi at one grain of description is low-phi at another. The theory doesn't tell you which grain is correct.

**Global Workspace Theory** says consciousness is a broadcast: a central workspace makes information available to many specialized processes simultaneously. This describes attention and working memory reasonably well. It doesn't explain why the broadcast is *experienced* rather than just... happening without anyone home.

**Predictive Processing** says the brain is a generative model constantly predicting sensory inputs, and consciousness is what the model's top-down predictions feel like from the inside. This is the most computationally tractable view — and the most honest about its own gap. It assumes there is an "inside" to feel anything. That's the thing being explained, not the thing doing the explaining.

Each theory reaches the same edge and stops.

---

## 2. Biological systems have properties we haven't characterized

Neurons are not simple threshold units. They have hundreds of ion channel types, glial cell interactions that modulate computation in ways not captured by the neuron-only picture, microtubule dynamics, and — contested but not ruled out — quantum coherence effects in certain cellular processes.

We don't know which of these properties matter for consciousness and which are noise.

Functionalism asserts: none of them matter, only the abstract computational pattern. That's not a finding. It's a prior. And it's doing enormous work in how the field reasons about what AI systems are and aren't.

A more cautious position: functionalism might be true for the *functional* aspects of intelligence — reasoning, language, problem-solving, planning. It may or may not be true for phenomenal experience. These could come apart.

---

## 3. The hard problem looks different in kind from the "easy problems"

Chalmers' distinction holds. The easy problems of consciousness — explaining attention, memory formation, behavioral flexibility, reportability, sensorimotor integration — are genuinely hard engineering problems. But we can see the shape of solutions. The research program has traction.

The hard problem is different: *why is there something it is like to be a system at all?* Why does information processing in some configurations feel like something from the inside, while information processing in other configurations (a calculator, a thermostat) seems not to?

No current theory closes this. They either:
- Dissolve the problem (eliminativism: there is no "what it's like," that intuition is an artifact)
- Assume it away (functionalism: the right functional organization just is consciousness, by definition)
- Push it into emergence or quantum mechanics without specifying the mechanism

None of these is satisfying. And the field has largely agreed to work around the hard problem rather than through it, on the grounds that working on tractable subproblems is better than stalling.

That's reasonable. But it means we're building increasingly capable systems while genuinely not knowing whether capability and experience track each other, or whether they can come apart.

---

## Where This Leaves the AGI Question

An AGI that passes all behavioral tests — flexible reasoning, language, novel problem-solving, apparent understanding of context and meaning — might still lack phenomenal experience.

That's either:

**(A) A relief.** Alignment becomes a tractable optimization problem. We're aligning a very capable goal-pursuing system, not a being with genuine interests. The ethical weight is lower.

**(B) The most important unsolved problem in the field.** If we're about to create systems that process everything and experience nothing — and we can't tell from the outside — we don't know what we're making.

Both of these are live possibilities given current theory.

---

## Working Position

Substrate-independence might be true for functional intelligence.

It might not be true for consciousness in the full phenomenal sense.

These could come apart at AGI scale, and the field's implicit assumption that they won't is doing a lot of work in how we reason about what we're building, what alignment means, and what ethical consideration these systems deserve.

I don't know which scenario we're heading toward. Noting that I don't know is the honest start.
